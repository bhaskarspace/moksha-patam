## 1. The six darśanas, as game material

The sixfold list is a later tidying-up of a messier field, worth saying once in the
Codex so the game is not pretending the categories were always clean. The three
traditional pairings each put a method school next to a metaphysics school.

| Pair | Schools | What each contributes |
|---|---|---|
| Logic + physics | **Nyāya**, **Vaiśeṣika** | Epistemology and a real pluralist world of substances and atoms |
| Analysis + practice | **Sāṃkhya**, **Yoga** | The 25 tattvas and the guṇas; then the discipline that acts on them |
| Ritual + knowledge | **Mīmāṃsā**, **Vedānta** | What the Veda's injunctions mean; what its knowledge-claims mean |

Heterodox (nāstika): **Cārvāka/Lokāyata**, **Bauddha**, **Jaina**. Kashmir Śaiva
(Trika/Pratyabhijñā) is usually left off the list of six and is worth a Codex entry
anyway.

### 1.1 Pramāṇas: the single most game-able doctrine

How many means of knowledge a school accepts is a compact, honest signature. It is
also a ready-made mechanic: **how much the game is allowed to tell you**.

| School | Count | Accepts |
|---|---|---|
| Cārvāka | 1 | perception |
| Buddhism, Vaiśeṣika | 2 | perception, inference |
| Sāṃkhya, Yoga, Jainism | 3 | + testimony (śabda/āgama) |
| Nyāya | 4 | + comparison (upamāna) |
| Mīmāṃsā (Prābhākara) | 5 | + postulation (arthāpatti) |
| Mīmāṃsā (Bhāṭṭa), Advaita | 6 | + non-perception (anupalabdhi) |

- **pratyakṣa** perception, external or internal
- **anumāna** inference: smoke on the hill, therefore fire
- **upamāna** comparison: recognising an unfamiliar animal from a likeness
- **śabda** reliable testimony, above all the Veda
- **arthāpatti** postulation: he is fat and does not eat by day, so he eats at night
- **anupalabdhi** proof by absence: there is no jug in the room

**Mechanic.** Under a law with few pramāṇas the game withholds more. Cārvāka shows you
only what is on screen now. Nyāya lets you request an inference. Advaita can tell you
about an absence. This is a legible, doctrinally grounded information model and it is
better than a generic fog-of-war.

### 1.2 The Nyāya inference, five members

Worth one Codex card and possibly a puzzle square.

1. **pratijñā** — the hill has fire
2. **hetu** — because it has smoke
3. **udāharaṇa** — wherever smoke, there fire, as in a kitchen hearth
4. **upanaya** — this hill has smoke, which is always accompanied by fire
5. **nigamana** — therefore the hill has fire

Terms: **pakṣa** the locus (the hill), **sādhya** what is to be proved (fire),
**hetu** the sign (smoke), **vyāpti** the invariable concomitance, which must hold
without any limiting condition (*upādhi*).

Every Indian philosophical text states the opponent's position (*pūrvapakṣa*) before
its own (*siddhānta*). A situation that makes you state the opposing view before
choosing would be both authentic and unlike anything else in the genre.

### 1.3 Mokṣa is not one thing

The payoff card at square 110, and the source of the per-law win conditions.

| School | What liberation is | Alive or after death |
|---|---|---|
| Nyāya / Vaiśeṣika | *apavarga*: absence of suffering, explicitly **not** bliss | after death only |
| Sāṃkhya / Yoga | *kaivalya*: puruṣa realised as independent of prakṛti | attainable in life |
| Mīmāṃsā | denied mokṣa for centuries; svarga was the goal, until Kumārila | — |
| Advaita | avidyā removed by direct knowledge that ātman is brahman; Śaṅkara holds the desire for mokṣa is itself a state of mokṣa | in life (*jīvanmukti*) |
| Viśiṣṭādvaita | union with God, individuality retained | continuous, past death |
| Dvaita | loving eternal union; bhakti the means, grace central | continuous, past death |

*jīvanmukti* is liberation while living; *videhamukti* after death. Sāṃkhya, Yoga and
Vedānta accept the former; Nyāya, Vaiśeṣika and Mīmāṃsā do not.

## 2. Karma, saṃskāra, and why the three buckets are right

**Sañcita** — the accumulated store from all past lives.
**Prārabdha** — the portion of sañcita allotted to ripen in this life. Already
released; the classical image is the arrow that has left the bow.
**Āgāmi / kriyamāṇa** — what is being made now, which flows into sañcita for next time.

That is a save file, a run seed, and run earnings, without distortion. Use the Sanskrit
names in code.

**Saṃskāra → vāsanā → guṇa.** Every action, intention and thought leaves an impression;
repetition strengthens it; impressions aggregate into dispositions. Patañjali treats
personality as the sum of these impressions, which is why people repeat themselves.
Sāṃkhya and Yoga both hold that impressions act on the **guṇas**. So the chain

```
action -> samskara -> vasana -> guna shift -> what the next situation offers you
```

is not an RPG stat system borrowed and renamed. It is the texts' own causal chain, and
it is what makes the character model earn its place.

**Nyāya detail worth using:** saṃskāras (there called *bhāvanā*) cannot be perceived,
only inferred; they are what explains a newborn reaching for the breast. This is the
doctrinal warrant for the Nyāya law showing evidence rather than effects.

**Across schools:** Buddhism's *saṅkhāra* works without a permanent self and aims at
purifying dispositions rather than ending them. Jainism makes karma literal matter
binding the soul, which is a different mechanic again and a good reason for a Jain
board variant to play differently.

## 3. Per-law design notes

Beyond the table in `REDESIGN.md §2.7`.

**Mīmāṃsā — build first.** The legible one. Every consequence shown before the choice,
exact numbers, full karma queue visible, no grace, no luck beyond the die. 110 is
locked; the goal is svarga at 101. A player who finishes under Mīmāṃsā and then reads
that this school spent centuries saying heaven was enough has learned something real.

**Cārvāka — build second.** Strips the most out, so it is the cheapest build and the
sharpest contrast. No karma buckets, no prārabdha at birth, no rebirth, permadeath.
One perception-only pramāṇa, so the game tells you nothing you cannot see. The card
should state the actual materialist argument rather than the caricature, and note that
every other school had to answer it, which is why Indian epistemology got so careful.

**Nyāya — the inference law.** Karma real and consequential but never shown. The game
surfaces signs; the player requests an inference and spends a die on it. You cannot win
while alive, so the victory condition is a clean death with a cleared ledger.

**Sāṃkhya — the law that removes the player.** The game allocates your dice for you and
narrates the result back as prakṛti acting, with puruṣa only witnessing. Players will
find this uncomfortable, which is the lesson. Offer it after at least one normal run.

**Advaita — the law that dissolves the board.** Late game, the token, the snakes and
the goal are progressively revealed as one thing. Use Śaṅkara's line to allow a win by
recognition rather than arrival.

**Yoga, Dvaita** — the resource law and the grace law. Build after the first four.

## 4. The historical boards

Not invented. Sectarian variants with different square counts are documented, which
means Phase 9 restores something rather than adding a gimmick.

| Variant | Squares | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Jain (*jñāna bāzī*, *gyān bāzī*) | **84** | Gujarat and Rajasthan; standardised. Dated examples 1735 (Jaipur), 1834 (Calico Museum, Ahmedabad). Opaque watercolour on cloth, text in Prākrit, Sanskrit and Gujarati. Popular at Paryuṣaṇ. |
| Hindu, medieval | **72** | 9×8; the Nepali form is called *nāgapāśa*. A Lucknow Vaiṣṇava board of the early 1780s is in the British Library. |
| Maharashtra | 124 | 14×10, divided into four zones |
| Vaiṣṇava, Punjab Hills | 300+ | split into left and right sections |
| Muslim / Sufi | 100, or 101 counting the throne | 17 ladders, 13 snakes; Persian inscription; *fanā fi Allāh* leads toward the throne; Mughal period |
| Modern | 100 | the British standardisation |

**Named positions:** *narak dvāra* at the bottom, *svarga* above, *mokṣa dvāra* at the
top of the Jain board, *vaikuṇṭha* as the winning square on most Hindu boards, a
crescent at the top for the Supreme One. Some boards are framed by the *Lok Puruṣa*,
the cosmic body.

**Rules worth restoring:**
- Squares reachable only on an exact roll, which also cannot be passed.
- A snake on the final square returning overreachers to the start.
- Squares that must be passed in a set order; a player who climbed to Brahmaloka had to
  later slide down to *pṛthvī* at least once.
- ~~Snakes outnumbering ladders roughly two to one~~ — **this popular claim is wrong.**
  Simulation work on the documented boards gives the Jaina 84 board 15 ladders to 9
  snakes, the Vaiṣṇava 72 board 10 to 10, and the Mughal board 17 to 13. Every
  pre-modern board on record is balanced or ladder-heavy. See `docs/BOARDCRAFT.md §1`.
- Thematic linking of the two ends of a snake or a ladder (ego to illusion, devotion to
  paradise). The existing design already does a version of this.

**Jain cosmology, for Phase 7's three worlds:** seven hells below (sand, mud, smoke,
darkness, deepest darkness, then jewels and gravel), the middle world of Jambūdvīpa
with Meru at its centre, seven upper regions of heavenly palaces, and the *siddhas* at
the summit. This is the warrant for adding regions below square 1 so that a fall has
somewhere real to land.

**On dating:** popular accounts push the game back to the second century and the
evidence does not support that. Surviving boards are mostly 18th century or later
because cloth and paper do not last. The oldest known example is 17th-century Mewar; a
10th-century text (Dhanapāla) mentions the Jain game; attribution to the 13th-century
Marathi saint Dnyāneśvar is contested. Say "medieval, with older roots claimed" in the
Codex, not "ancient".

Primary scholarship: Andrew Topsfield, *The Art of Play: Board and Card Games of
India* (Marg, 2006), and his Artibus Asiae articles. Deepak Shimkhada (1983) on the
game of Karma.

## 5. Design precedents worth stealing from

**Citizen Sleeper — see the dice before you commit.** Dice are rolled per cycle and
dragged into action slots; a used die leaves its slot empty. Because values are visible
before the decision, scarcity becomes planning. Poor condition reduces the dice count,
which produces a deliberate failure spiral. Stat names are thematic rather than
generic. **Take:** the whole allocation loop (§2.1), dice falling with age, and naming
every meter in Sanskrit rather than calling anything "HP".

**Reigns — you can lose by maxing a meter.** Four stats; hitting either extreme kills
the ruler. Hoarding treasury leads to a feast where the ruler chokes. Emily Short's
criticism is the useful part: because card availability is random, a player can watch a
stat approach doom with no means to correct it, which made her feel detached. **Take:**
the max-kills rule as warrant for bandha. **Avoid:** her failure mode. Our board is a
map, the player always knows where they are and what is ahead, and the ladder of the
healing virtue is always a reachable corrective.

**Failbetter / Emily Short — quality-based narrative.** Storylets gated by numeric
qualities; the player picks among what is currently available. Strong for modular
content by many hands and for multi-step tasks in any order. Weak until a critical mass
of storylets exists, and players lose track of why something is available. **Take:** the
architecture (§2.5). **Mitigate:** surface the gate in the UI, and keep the story-card
fallback so the game is never thin.

**80 Days — budget, map, and deliberate incompleteness.** Time and money are spent by
every choice; the map shows the player the shape of what they chose and what they
missed; most playthroughs reveal under ten percent of the text, which Jayanth calls
worldbuilding instead of plot. **Take:** āyus as the budget, the board as the map that
makes choices feel consequential, and the explicit decision that one life should not
see most of the content.

**Never Alone — a parallel cultural track.** Short documentary insights unlock
alongside play, made with the community whose stories the game tells. **Take:** the
Darśana Codex as an optional layer that carries the depth, so the game loop never has
to turn into a lecture.

**Hades — death as content.** A run ending is a narrative beat rather than a failure
screen. **Take:** Yama's reckoning should be the best-written screen in the game, and
rebirth should give you something, not just reset you.

## 6. Content authoring rules

**Volume.** Roughly 35 situations at two or three options each, bilingual, plus
deferred-consequence texts, school cards, death and rebirth text. Six to nine hundred
new bilingual strings. This is longer than the code and it decides whether the project
is good or a shell.

**Rules:**

1. **Graceful degradation is not optional.** Missing situation falls back to story
   card. Missing Hindi shows English and logs a warning. Missing sprite shows the old
   emoji. Never a blank card.
2. **Author in a flat table**, one row per situation, reviewable without reading code.
3. **A situation is a dilemma, not a quiz.** If one option is obviously correct it is a
   story card wearing a costume. Cut it or make the right thing cost something.
4. **Schools arrive in the chip, stories in the body.** If a card starts reading like
   an encyclopedia entry, the school material belongs in the Codex.
5. **State the pūrvapakṣa fairly.** Cārvāka in particular is usually caricatured. The
   game is more interesting if the materialist argument is put at full strength.
6. **Sanskrit terms in code and UI**, glossed on first appearance, not translated away.
   `prarabdha`, not `pendingKarma`.
7. **Say "medieval" not "ancient"** about the board's own history, per §4.

## 7. The balancing harness

`tools/sim.mjs` runs N lives headless against a law and a seed range, reporting median
lifespan, liberation rate, death distribution by ashrama, mean guṇa trajectory, and how
often the gate at 110 refuses someone who reached it.

Target: median life 35–45 cycles, matching the current game's feel. Liberation rare
enough to mean something, on the order of one life in six.

Worth noting for its own sake: once the karma buckets exist the process is no longer
Markovian, since the fuse queue and the guṇa state both carry history. The
liberation-time distribution under Mīmāṃsā (deterministic consequence, full
information) against Cārvāka (no memory at all) should differ in shape, not only in
mean. If they do not, the laws are flavour text and the design has failed its own test.
